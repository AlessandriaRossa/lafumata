The Tuesday evening online meeting began with a review of articles from *[The Economist](https://www.economist.com/leaders/2026/10/08/will-bonds-blow-up)*, the *New York Times*, and the *Financial Times* on the debt crisis.

At the European level, tension linked to economic and social instability is growing: after Spain and France, Belgium and [Portugal](https://www.lindipendente.online/2026/10/12/anche-il-portogallo-e-sceso-in-piazza-contro-il-carovita/) have also joined the [social turmoil](https://www.quinterna.org/pubblicazioni/rivista/29/marasma_sociale_e_guerra.htm). In Belgium, there have been demonstrations against a budget measure aimed at restoring public finances by saving 10 billion euros within the end of the legislative term. The government's plan has sparked protests in the public sector, from transport to schools, and among students. In Portugal, marches took place in all the major cities, with an *acampada* in Porto, under the slogan: “We cannot live like this anymore”.

The economic crisis in France has turned into a social crisis. Italy has a debt-to-GDP ratio that has exceeded 130%, therefore higher than France's, but Paris is running a higher deficit (3.1% for Italy, 5.4% for France). The simplest way for a government to reduce the deficit—outflows exceeding income—is to cut public spending: this means raising the retirement age and reducing healthcare spending and *welfare* more generally. This amounts to pouring fuel on the fire. The recent strikes and demonstrations are the *product* of a critical situation, and they become a *factor* of further criticality, since they send government bond yields soaring. For *The Economist* (“[The countries most threatened by turbulent bond markets](https://www.economist.com/briefing/2026/10/08/the-countries-most-threatened-by-turbulent-bond-markets)”), the most worrying force pushing bond yields upward is political and fiscal risk. For the *FT* (“[France: between the bond market and the barricades](https://www.ft.com/content/9b252b46-a87c-45e7-a09a-45a39ce077b8?syn-25a6b1a6=1)”):

> *“France’s political gridlock and worsening debt have made things very different. A series of ratings downgrades has helped push up the country’s borrowing costs, which passed Greece’s in late 2024 and Italy’s last year. [...]”*

The energy *shock* caused by the war in the Middle East and in Ukraine has led to an increase in the cost of crude oil of about 40% over the past year, and also in the cost of gas and fertilisers. Countries that import energy are obviously the most exposed. Before the war waged by the United States and Israel against Iran, the IMF expected a slight slowdown in inflation, which instead increased. This is a problem that immediately affects the purchasing power of wages and concerns Europe, but also the United Kingdom, the USA, and Japan. The *NYT* writes in an article titled “[France Is Veering Toward a Potential Debt Crisis, a Warning to the World](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/08/business/france-bond-yields.html)”:

> *“In Asia, Japan’s debt trajectory recently tested the nerve of the market. Japan’s debt has been more than twice the size of its economy for many years, but recent government promises to increase spending and cut taxes have punished Japan’s financial assets. The yen weakened so much that the U.S. Treasury supported an intervention to bolster the currency, and 10-year Japanese bond yields are trading at their highest levels in three decades.”*

The ECB could intervene with its *bazooka* in support of France, but this would create a dangerous precedent, which would lead other countries to loosen fiscal discipline. From whatever angle one looks at the economic situation, there are no *exit strategies* for capitalism. The only way to reduce excessive deficits and lower public debt would be economic growth, that is, an increase in GDP. Italy too is beginning to have problems managing the interest on its debt, and indeed it is kept under scrutiny by the markets. A country can live with high debt if the economy grows rapidly, but when interest rates exceed growth, a spiral is set in motion that can spin out of control.

Behind the protests in Europe, therefore, lies a series of skewed economic parameters. Anyone who goes into government today finds themselves grappling with reduced margins for manoeuvre. The growth of populist parties is due to disillusionment with the existing political framework. What has been gripping capitalism since 2008 (though one could go back to 1975) is not a conjunctural crisis. The problems cannot be solved: they are allowed to grow out of proportion, and this leads to the crumbling of States, which are increasingly less able to intervene at the economic and political level. In Spain, prime minister Sánchez has dissolved Parliament and called early elections; in France, voters will go to the polls in a complicated political situation; in Germany, the political system is increasingly fragile.

The capitalistic mode of production rests on the production of surplus value; but if dead labor grows disproportionately relative to living labor, it is precisely this production that is undermined. Contemporary capitalism takes to its extreme consequences the tendency described by Marx: the more one invests, the more the rate of profit falls.

Finance somehow manages to conceal this problem: as long as there are solid expectations about future returns, the merry-go-round keeps spinning. For example, Nvidia supports the construction of OpenAI's new *data centres*, which will buy Nvidia chips for its AI models. The money invested thus returns to its starting point, and meanwhile appears among Nvidia's revenues. Enormous masses of capital allow this type of company to increase its stock market valuation. Companies invest in each other's development, feeding a [bubble](https://valori.it/accordo-nvidia-openai-bolla-debito-ia/) destined to burst.

Once a certain threshold is exceeded, the gap between advanced capital and achievable profits becomes too wide, and so investors synchronise on the fateful question: is it worth [investing](https://www.economist.com/briefing/2026/10/08/the-countries-most-threatened-by-turbulent-bond-markets) in something that could go up in smoke? The 2008 *subprime* mortgage crisis clearly showed the mechanism that leads investors to get rid of securities perceived as toxic.

Today, what drives “growth” are the big tech companies, in turn supported by the large investment funds that administer pensions and insurance. When this bubble bursts, the consequences will be catastrophic for the market, for States, and for so-called consumers. If for Marx the falling rate of profit was a tendency opposed by counteracting causes, by now all such “tricks” have been extensively used up. The productivity achieved can hardly grow any further: one cannot extract from a single worker the same [surplus value](https://www.quinterna.org/pubblicazioni/rivista/19/automazione_totale.htm) that is extracted from a hundred or a thousand.

War is by no means a revitalising element for capital: one third of the world's helium, indispensable in chip production, and half of the maritime trade in sulfur pass through the Strait of Hormuz. AI, the internet, and *data centres* cannot function unless maritime routes are efficient and the straits navigable.

[Debt](https://www.usdebtclock.org/world-debt-clock.html), war, social turmoil: it all holds together. In the *Diagram of the Relative Increases in Industrial Production of the Major Countries from 1914 to 2008*, reproduced in the article “[Un modello dinamico di crisi](https://www.quinterna.org/pubblicazioni/rivista/24/modello_dinamico_01.htm)”[^1] (*n*+1, no. 24), one can notice the thinning of industrial production growth, and therefore of the rate of profit. All the major economies are moving toward the asymptote, and for capitalism, non-growth means death.

Europe cannot afford the collapse of France, because it would set off a domino effect. Markets will therefore continue to be flooded with money, and inflation will continue to rise. The title of the film *[Too Big to Fail](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NTNobGwKIu8)* (2011) referred to the big banks (it was 2008), but in the meantime it is the most important States that are facing [systemic problems](https://www.quinterna.org/pubblicazioni/rivista/24/capitalismo_che_nega_s%C3%A9_stesso.htm). The USA cannot let OpenAI, Anthropic, or Palantir fail: [militarily and economically](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69x7E2iNevQ), these companies are fundamental resources in the competition with China.

For decades the USA has assumed the role of a *rentier* country and [defends](https://www.quinterna.org/pubblicazioni/rivista/11/invasione_iraq.htm) that position in order not to collapse: American gross public debt exceeds 40 trillion dollars, over 120% of GDP, and the annual deficit is bound to grow. But its political-military activism accelerates the crisis into which it has plunged – part of a more general crisis affecting capitalism as a whole.

The recent street movements rail against high rents, against the rising cost of living, against cuts to public spending; they have a confused vocabulary, borrowed from the adversary, and they do not clearly know who the “enemy” is. But they express [anti-system](https://www.quinterna.org/lingue/english/historical_en/layout_work_programme.htm) demands, that is, demands that cannot be obtained within the present social form. The idea is beginning to gain ground that the problem is not the fair distribution of wealth, but doing away with this mode of production, which represents a serious danger for the human species.
