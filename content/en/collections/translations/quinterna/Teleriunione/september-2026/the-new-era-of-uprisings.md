The Tuesday evening online meeting began by revisiting Joshua Clover's essay *Riot. Strike. Riot. The New Era of Uprisings* (2023), [reviewed](https://www.quinterna.org/pubblicazioni/rivista/55/dallo-sciopero-alla-rivolta-alla-comune.htm) in issue no. 55 of the periodical *n*+1.

The text starts from the struggles of the 1700s in order to outline its central thesis on the nature of class conflict in different historical periods. The first phase, that of the riots in England, begins in the countryside, is due to capitalist primitive accumulation, develops around maritime ports and large fairs, in the places of commodity circulation, and involves populations that an economic transformation literally reduces to hunger. With the subsequent development of capitalism, there is a progressive shift of struggles from the places of circulation to those of production, the factories, where the strike becomes the weapon for improving the living conditions of wage earners. The third and final phase coincides with the processes of deindustrialisation, especially in the advanced economies (Europe and the USA), and brings the struggle back to the territory. The expulsion of labour power from industry brings with it the impossibility of survival for millions of workers (think of the uprisings of Black Americans in the 1960s). Thus we arrive at open confrontation with the repressive apparatuses that intervene to defend the *status quo*. It is no coincidence, therefore, that in recent decades protesters have permanently occupied streets and squares (the Arab Springs, the Acampadas, Occupy Wall Street, etc.).

According to Clover, the formation of the Oakland Commune (2011–2012) is an emblematic case both for recovering the historical memory of the Paris Commune and for the attempt to bring to life relations different from those that exist. The revolts of the so-called [Generation Z](https://www.chicago86.org/lotte-in-corso/internazionale/1426-l-autunno-caldo-della-generazione-z) connect to what is written in the text, especially with regard to the growth of the surplus population—the army of the reserveless—on a planetary scale.

Riot 1.0, which also passes through Luddism, is a first delineation of the class confrontation, followed by the cycle of the strike and, finally, by revolt 2.0, which manifests itself in the phase of the historical [crisis](https://www.quinterna.org/pubblicazioni/rivista/41/il_grande_collasso.htm) of senile capitalism and avails itself of extremely powerful means of coordination. The schema proposed by Clover is effective, but it should be read with *fuzzy* logic: strikes have not disappeared—he himself acknowledges this, seeing them becoming territorial—and the proletariat continues to fight to defend its living conditions. Rather, we have moved to a higher level, in which the strike is subsumed into a broader dynamic.

Already from the early 1920s, the “Italian” Communist Left fought against the directive to occupy factories, counterposing the instruction to [organise territorially](https://www.quinterna.org/archivio/1911_1920/prendere_fabbrica.htm) and to occupy the places of bourgeois power. In the article “[L'operaismo italiano e il suo Sessantotto lungo vent'anni](https://www.quinterna.org/pubblicazioni/rivista/14/operaismo_italiano.htm)”[^1] (*n*+1, no. 14), we traced a continuity between Gramscian ordinovism and the workerism of the 1960s and 1970s. Today the correctness of the Left's instruction is evident: the proletariat has spread across the territory, the wage earner works side by side with other wage earners for increasingly shorter periods, under conditions of [growing precarity](https://www.quinterna.org/pubblicazioni/rivista/40/gig_economy.htm). In China, flexibly employed workers—*gig workers*—number over 200 million, and many move from industry to services, performing a myriad of “odd jobs”. Permanent positions, contracts, and the cult of work tend to disappear, while even the figure of the flesh-and-blood boss gives way to an algorithm. To work, one increasingly often needs to access *online* platforms via *apps*, and therefore millions of workers have become familiar with these technologies. What is the proletariat today? It is certainly the factory worker, but also the reserveless person who will never work, or the precarious worker holding two or three jobs in order to survive.

Machines, which under capitalism appear alien to us (even though they speak to us through *chatbots*), are not the problem but part of the solution, since our [evolution](https://www.quinterna.org/pubblicazioni/rivista/19/genesi_uomo_industria.htm) is corporeal and extracorporeal at the same time, as the hand-labour-brain *feedback* shows.

In the essay *The Great Resignation. The New Refusal of Work* (2023), [reviewed](https://www.quinterna.org/pubblicazioni/rivista/54/tendenza-antiwork.htm) in issue no. 54 of our periodical, Francesca Coin analyses the phenomenon of millions of workers worldwide, especially young people, who are calling into question the logic of working at all costs, giving rise to forms of refusal of the existing order (Antiwork, Tǎng Píng, etc.).

In Madrid, following the eviction of an elderly woman, a new Acampada in Puerta del Sol began, 15 years after the 2011 one, against high rents and the housing crisis. The revolts of recent years show a tendency to take to the streets to seek *something else* rather than to demand something. The latest demonstrations take on the typical characteristics of *swarm intelligence*, thanks also to the massive use of *social networks*.

Millions of human beings, interacting with one another, can produce unexpected, *emergent* behaviour. This recalls “our” schema of the [reversal of praxis](https://www.quinterna.org/pubblicazioni/rivista/19/rovesciamento_prassi.htm): physiological drives lead “social atoms” to give themselves new configurations and to head, without full awareness of it, toward an “attractor” placed in the future.

The historical dynamic proceeds in leaps, through discontinuities and bifurcations. In the article “[In senso lato e in senso stretto](https://www.quinterna.org/pubblicazioni/rivista/39/in_senso_lato_e_in_senso_stretto.htm)”[^2] (*n*+1, no. 39), we saw that in society there are always overlapping sets, and that any complex system changes in response to the *inputs* it receives from its environment. Already today we see demonstrations that tend to synchronise worldwide, through *social media*, websites, and platforms. Among the dissolutions currently underway is that of the movement *leader*, since “social atoms” polarise and self-organise without the need for someone to direct them.

Not only industry and the means of communication have changed: the world has changed. Demonstrations will not reappear with the same forms or the same language as in the past, and this is why we criticise Third-Internationalism. For the Left, revolution is not a question of form, but of force.

Adam Greenfield, in the essay *Lifehouse: Taking Care of Ourselves in a World on Fire*, ([reviewed](https://www.quinterna.org/pubblicazioni/rivista/56/cio-che-sembrava-un-mezzo-e-diventato-lo-scopo.htm) in issue no. 56 of the periodical *n*+1), recounts the birth of a mutual aid network after Hurricane Sandy struck the East Coast of the United States. For the author, the Occupy Sandy movement (2012) was the culmination of a process that had begun years earlier: in the face of disasters and the State's inability to intervene, structures of mutual help form entirely spontaneously. According to Greenfield, similar experiences had already been seen after Hurricane Katrina (2005), and this happens because we live in a world without shelter and without hospitality, in which the old economic and political structures are crumbling: *“[...] many of the systems and structures that currently shape our lives will be flung to pieces by the force of events”.*

The new [paradigm](https://www.quinterna.org/pubblicazioni/rivista/26/struttura_frattale.htm) makes its way through society: first it is fought against, then it finds space in small minorities, and finally it imposes itself when the situation is ripe (Thomas Kuhn, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*). The State, with all its power, is unable to adapt to the development of the productive forces and becomes an obstacle to their further development. The only compass that allows us to orient ourselves in a context of “social turmoil and war” is the Left's method of work, based on the yesterday-today-tomorrow dynamic (*On the Thread of Time*). Climate crisis, economic crisis, precarity, unemployment, inflation, war, “meaningless life”: there is no shortage of reasons to take to the streets. Yet the generalised crisis of capitalism is not enough for the *anti-form* to impose itself: revolution is not materially possible unless the repressive structure of the dominant class collapses from within.
